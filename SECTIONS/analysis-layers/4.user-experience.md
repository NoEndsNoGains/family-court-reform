# 6.4 Layer 4: User Experience – Litigants in Person (LiPs)

**Purpose**: This layer examines how design failures (Layer 1), judicial gatekeeping (Layer 2), and professional filtering (Layer 3) manifest as lived barriers in user help-seeking behaviour. It draws on **unpaid, real-world questions submitted by the public to legal experts on the JustAnswer Q&A platform (n=23, 2004–Nov 2025)** to surface **the experiential reality** of transcript access and publication pathways for those transparency reforms are meant to serve. HMCTS collects no data on transcript requests, refusals, timelines, or user difficulties. The system does not record how users experience it. What users ask strangers on the internet for paid legal advice becomes, by default, the systematic window into lived experience.

**Positioning in five-layer framework**: Layer 4 captures unmediated user encounters with the system—particularly litigants in person (LiPs) who lack the tacit knowledge, strategic timing, and cost navigation that representation provides. **The layer functions as the system's unmediated stress test,** capturing the point where the court's assumptions about user competence crash against the practical reality of the unrepresented individual. The analytical power of this layer **is sharpest when litigants are also first-time users**—encountering procedures, forms, and deadlines without prior experience, learned workarounds, or accumulated adaptations that typically mask design flaws. When someone uses a system for the first time without professional guidance, every gap between design intention and practical usability becomes visible. What professionals navigate through tacit knowledge, first-time users collide with directly. Layer 4 documents those collisions—because only by documenting them can we see clearly where the gaps are. And only when we see clearly where the gaps are can we not only evaluate and respond to them, but ask the more fundamental question: why do these gaps exist in the first place?

**Unique contribution**: Layer 4 provides triangulation across the framework. It tests whether the barriers documented architecturally (Layer 1), observed judicially (Layer 2), and mediated professionally (Layer 3) are isolated phenomena or interconnected patterns. When unmediated users—without professional guidance, without learned adaptations—encounter the same barriers that architectural analysis predicted, the convergence is validation. But Layer 4 is more than validation. It is the sharpest test: because there is no professional buffer, no accumulated workaround, no tacit knowledge softening the collision. What users hit, they hit directly. If gaps exist, Layer 4 will find them.

**Scope**: The analysis draws on a corpus of n=23 procedurally detailed threads from JustAnswer.co.uk (2004–November 2025), filtered from an initial 75 threads for specificity and relevance. The inquiry focused on user encounters with transcript access processes; the specific barrier categories—EX107 procedures, funding pathway confusion, publication restrictions, and recording integrity—emerged inductively from thematic analysis rather than being imposed in advance.

## 6.4.1 LiPs as Primary Users, Not Exceptions (The Critical Frame)

The 2012 Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act (LASPO), implemented in April 2013, removed legal aid from most private family law cases. Before LASPO, separating couples who met financial eligibility criteria could access publicly funded legal representation for disputes about children and finances. After LASPO, legal aid remained available only in cases involving evidence of domestic abuse or child abuse—leaving the majority of private family law litigants without access to funded representation (Ministry of Justice, 2014). The consequence was a structural transformation: since LASPO, around 80% of private family law cases have involved at least one unrepresented party (ACSO, 2022; Ministry of Justice, 2024). **Litigants in Person (LiPs) are no longer an exception**; they are now the core user base of the family courts. Over the same period, more than 100,000 children were trapped in family court backlogs in 2023 (Law Society, 2024), and these delays are frequently linked to the growth in LiPs, who are routinely portrayed as unprepared, emotional, and time-consuming for court staff (Trinder et al., 2014; Tomlinson et al., 2022).

Yet this framing does more than misplace responsibility; **it recasts the unrepresented user as the source of dysfunction in a system that was not built around them**. For much of English legal history, a range of courts—including manorial, borough, and ecclesiastical tribunals—heard lay people directly: peasants, tenants, traders, and villagers brought disputes in their own words, often emotional and inarticulate by modern legal standards (Briggs and Schofield, 2020; University of Cambridge, 2025). Judges and local officials were expected to question, probe, and clarify, and the procedures of these courts developed on the assumption that ordinary people would appear without a professional intermediary (Doncaster Heritage Services, n.d.). The distinct, fully professionalised roles of barrister and solicitor emerged gradually from the later Middle Ages, with solicitors "soliciting causes" in Chancery and other courts by the 16th and 17th centuries and a more rigid division between courtroom advocates and client-facing solicitors crystallising over the 18th and 19th centuries (Baker, 2019; Baker, 2002). The modern adversarial model—in which highly trained lawyers conduct the case in specialist language, and the courtroom is procedurally oriented around exchanges between professionals—is therefore a relatively recent design choice, not an ancient necessity. Justice, it seems, became too majestic to hear from ordinary people directly; their voices now required professional translation before they could be admitted to the courtroom. Against that backdrop, claims that contemporary judges "cannot" handle unrepresented litigants say less about human capacity for judging and more about how the current institutional architecture has been engineered to assume the constant presence of professional intermediaries (Law Society, 2022; ACSO, 2022).

That professionalised design created a triangle: Judge—Solicitor—Litigant. The Judge and Solicitor share professional language, training, and habitus; procedures are built around their relationship. The Solicitor translates the Litigant's emotional, unstructured experience into legal argument—absorbing distress, navigating procedure, protecting against procedural traps. The Judge rarely deals with the Litigant directly; the system assumes the Solicitor is always in between.

LASPO did not remove the Solicitor from the system—it removed the funding that connected the Solicitor to the Litigant. All three points of the triangle still exist. Solicitors still practise; judges still preside; litigants still appear. But in 80% of private family cases, at least one party now appears without the Solicitor connection. For those litigants, what remains is not a functioning triangle but a broken line: Judge on one side, Litigant on the other, with no translator between them. The directionality of communication collapses. The Judge speaks toward where the system was designed to place the Solicitor. The Litigant speaks toward where the system was designed to place the Solicitor. Neither reaches the other directly. The Litigant must now perform the Solicitor's role—translating their own distress into legal procedure, knowing the forms, meeting the deadlines—without training, knowledge, or professional habitus. When they cannot, they are blamed and they fail. 

This is not simply a return to ancient conditions. In early legal systems—Roman courts, Greek assemblies, English manorial tribunals—the litigant stood directly before the judge, vulnerable but not abandoned. The system was designed around that vulnerability. Judges probed, clarified, adapted. Payment, where it existed, was for access to the court, not for translation of one's distress into legal language. The professionalised system changed this fundamentally: the Solicitor became the translator and buffer, and Legal Aid was the mechanism that paid for that translation on behalf of those who could not afford it. The system then redesigned itself around the assumption that translation would always occur. LASPO removed the funding for translation but left the translated language intact. The LiP is thrust back into a position of direct vulnerability—but facing a system that no longer adapts to the unrepresented user. The procedures still assume professional intermediation. The forms still speak solicitor-to-court. The timelines still presuppose someone who knows when to act. The tragedy is not merely that the translator was removed or at least least used; it is that the system forgot it ever learned to hear ordinary people directly.

**This leaves a fundamental design question we are foced to ask: can we reimagine a system where interaction between Judge (Justice) and Litigant is effective not only when mediated but also when direct?**


This is not an aspirational question. LASPO already answered it. When 80% of family court users appear without professional representation, the system no longer has a choice about whether to serve unmediated users—only whether to do so effectively or continue to fail.
What it requires now is leadership: the courage to notice these failures, to name them, and to act. But it also requires something harder—a shift in how we see the relationship between the court and its users. The medieval illumination places judges at the top, ordinary people at the bottom. Power descends. But turn the picture upside down. Without the people seeking resolution, there is no need for judges at all. The court's authority does not flow from its majesty; it flows from the people it exists to serve. A system that forgets this—that designs around professional convenience rather than user need—has inverted its own purpose.
But courage without precision is just noise. To design solutions that actually close gaps, we must first identify those gaps with rigour. Otherwise we are guessing—imagining problems that may not exist, or designing responses to what we believe the problems are rather than what they actually are. The only way to know where the system fails is to look at where users collide with it. That is what the analysis that follows provides: not theory about what might be wrong, but evidence of what is.
Layer 4 analysis treats LiP "failures" as diagnostic signals of what must change. The sections that follow present the evidence.

## 6.4.2 Data Collection and Sampling Strategy

Preliminary scoping identified three potential sources of user-generated procedural accounts: JustAnswer.co.uk, Mumsnet, and Reddit /r/LegalAdviceUK. Systematic Google and Ecosia searches using core queries ("family court" transcript, EX107, transcript AND ("cost" OR "Legal Aid")) revealed that JustAnswer generated the highest volume of procedurally detailed, legally moderated threads. Search results consistently returned 8–10 JustAnswer threads per query compared to 1–2 scattered posts across other platforms. 

Given JustAnswer's advantages—verified legal expert responses, timestamped queries, and consistent procedural focus—analytical resources were concentrated on this single, richest data source rather than fragmentary coverage across multiple platforms.

Searches covered the full temporal range from 2004 (JustAnswer UK launch) to November 2025, including date-specific queries targeting pre-2020 content. The finding that 87% of relevant threads cluster post-2020 reflects a simple reality: before EX107 rollout (2017) and transparency reforms (2021–2023), the pathway for users to request transcripts barely existed. The law created the right—and with it, created the conditions for users to encounter the barriers. Problems did not intensify; they became visible because users could finally attempt what was previously inaccessible.

**Inclusion criteria:**  
England & Wales family proceedings; parent or litigant-in-person perspective; substantive reference to transcript access, EX107 applications, publication barriers, or related confidentiality/appeal issues.

**Exclusions:**  
Criminal matters, duplicates, generic complaints without procedural detail, non-transcript procedures.

Threads were identified through iterative searches across Google and Ecosia using query combinations (e.g., `site:justanswer.co.uk "family court" transcript EX107`) and date-restricted searches (`before:2020-01-01`) to test temporal distribution. Search queries and resulting URLs are logged in the research repository.

Searches yielded 75 unique threads. After excluding non-family-law content, 55 remained. Of these, **23 directly addressed** transcript access, EX107 applications, publication barriers, or related appeals/confidentiality issues—forming the final analytical corpus. Of these 23, **17 were directly relevant** to transparency and transcript access barriers; 6 addressed general family law procedural matters not directly relevant to the research questions.

**Temporal distribution (n=23):**

| Period        | n  | %   | Focus                                      |
|---------------|----|-----|--------------------------------------------|
| Pre-2020      | 3  | 13% | Admissibility and legacy procedures        |
| 2020–2023     | 14 | 61% | Concentration following transparency reforms |
| 2024–2025     | 6  | 26% | Ongoing struggles post-open reporting provisions |

This post-2020 concentration (87%) aligns with EX107 rollout (2017) and transparency reforms (2021–2023), validating the hypothesis that user-reported struggles intensified as new procedures were implemented without corresponding infrastructure improvements.

Although the primary focus is family proceedings, threads from analogous civil contexts (e.g., small claims in Thread 5) were included where the transcript request process via EX107 mirrors that in family courts, highlighting shared procedural challenges.

## 6.4.3 Analytical Method

Each of the 23 threads was read and coded for two things: what the user was struggling with, and what the expert told them.

**User queries were coded for:**
- **Primary problem**: The core issue that prompted them to seek help (transcript access, appeal process, recording problem, publication/confidentiality)
- **Process stage**: Where in the process they sought help (pre-filing, post-EX107, post-receipt, post-hearing, during appeal)
- **Specific indicators**: Whether they mentioned delay, cost, appeal deadline pressure, recording problems, publication concerns, or form confusion

**Expert responses were coded for:**
- Whether they addressed the user's core problem
- Whether they warned about common obstacles (delays, costs, deadlines)
- Whether they mentioned transcript access (EX107) when relevant to appeals

**Coding decisions:**
- **Delay** was coded as present only when the user explicitly complained about waiting or timeline (e.g., "stuck for over a month," "took 4 months"). References to time periods describing other problems (e.g., "3 months later the judge refused") were not coded as delay complaints.
- **Cost** was coded when the user or expert explicitly mentioned payment, pricing, or funding.
- **Appeal deadline pressure** was coded when the user was post-hearing wanting to appeal, already appealing, or explicitly mentioned deadline concern.

**Sample composition:**  
Of 23 threads, 17 (74%) directly addressed transcript access, recording issues, publication/confidentiality, or appeal-transcript dependency. Six threads addressed general family law procedural matters not directly relevant to transparency and were excluded from thematic analysis.

## 6.4.5 Thematic Analysis: What Users Ask About

### 6.4.5.1 Gap Indicators: What Problems Users Encountered

Among the 17 directly relevant threads, the following problems were identified (categories not mutually exclusive):

| Gap Indicator            | n | %   | Threads                  | Example                                                                 |
|--------------------------|---|-----|--------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Appeal deadline pressure | 9 | 53% | 4, 6, 7, 9, 17, 19, 20, 21, 23 | "Disagree with a final order... what can I do?" → Expert: "File N161 within 21 days. Order transcript via EX107." (Thread 19) |
| Form/process confusion   | 6 | 35% | 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 22       | "What does final approval mean, and why would it be stuck?" (Thread 3); "Which form do I need?" (Thread 8) |
| Recording problems       | 3 | 18% | 2, 4, 7                 | "The transcriber said most of the recording was inaudible... content has been changed" (Thread 4) |
| Publication/confidentiality | 3 | 18% | 1, 12, 18              | "My ex is sharing private and confidential information only disclosed in court documents" (Thread 12) |
| Timeline/delay mentioned | 2 | 12% | 3, 4                   | "Stuck on final approval for over a month" (Thread 3); "Took 4 months with many delays" (Thread 4) |
| Cost mentioned           | 2 | 12% | 2, 5                   | Expert reveals per-folio pricing only after user asks general "how to get transcript" question (Thread 5) |

**Critical finding:** The dominant issue is **appeal deadline pressure** (53%), not timeline confusion (12%). Users are not primarily asking "how long will this take?"—they are asking "I need to appeal **now** and I don't have a transcript yet." This highlights a **structural collision** between what an effective appeal requires and what the transcript system can deliver within the court's own deadlines.

### 6.4.5.2 The Absence of Delay and Cost Complaints

Only 2 of 17 threads (12%) explicitly mentioned delay, and only 2 (12%) mentioned cost. This low visibility does **not** mean delays and costs are minor problems. Instead, it suggests:

1. **Delay becomes visible only after commitment**  
   Both threads mentioning delay (3 & 4) are **post-EX107**—users discover the problem only after they have already applied and started waiting.
2. **Cost is invisible until quoted**  
   In Thread 5 (general "how do I get a transcript?" query), the expert reveals per-folio charging that the user had no prior awareness of. Users cannot complain about costs they have not yet been told exist.
3. **JustAnswer captures the moment of crisis, not anticipation**  
   Users post when they hit a wall (appeal clock ticking). At that point the urgent problem is the **immediate deadline**, not future delay or eventual cost.

**Conclusion:** The low prevalence of explicit delay and cost complaints does **not** contradict evidence from Layers 1–3 about systemic delays and high costs. It shows that users encounter these barriers **at different stages** than the moment they seek urgent online help.

### 6.4.5.3 What Users Did Not Know

The threads reveal specific knowledge gaps—not assumed misconceptions, but demonstrable absences of information that caused users to seek help:

| Knowledge Gap                                    | Evidence from Threads                                                                 | Threads       |
|--------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------|
| Transcripts must be requested separately for appeals | Users post-hearing asked "what can I do?" and were told by experts to order transcript via EX107. The need was not self-evident. | 17, 19, 21, 23 |
| "Final approval" stage exists and has no timeline | Thread 3 user explicitly asked "What does final approval mean, and why would it be stuck?" | 3             |
| Which form to use                                | Users asked "which form do I need?" or were confused between multiple forms (N244, N161, EX107, C110A, D11) | 5, 7, 8, 9, 22 |
| What happens when recordings fail                | Thread 4 user received inaudible recording and asked how to challenge. Thread 7 user asked about retrial when transcript unavailable. No user knew a fallback protocol. | 4, 7          |
| Publication restrictions on transcripts          | Thread 1 user didn't know they needed judicial permission to use family court documents in civil proceedings. Thread 12 user didn't know how to enforce confidentiality when ex-partner breached it. | 1, 12         |
| Cost structure                                   | Thread 5 user asked basic "how do I get a transcript" question; expert revealed per-folio pricing. User had no prior knowledge of cost. | 5             |

**What the data does not show:**  
The threads do not provide direct evidence of users believing "48-hour service means quick delivery" or "Help with Fees covers vendor costs." These may be real misconceptions, but this sample does not capture users articulating such beliefs.

**Implication:** Knowledge gaps cluster around process stages that official forms do not explain—what happens after filing (approval void), what appeals require (transcript dependency), what to do when recordings fail (no protocol). Users know enough to file; they don't know what comes next.

### 6.4.5.4 Emotional and Material Impacts

#### User Emotional State

Among the 17 directly relevant threads, user emotional tone was inferred from language:

| Emotional Tone          | n | %   | Threads             |
|-------------------------|---|-----|---------------------|
| Neutral/information-seeking | 4 | 24% | 1, 5, 18, 22       |
| Confused/anxious        | 3 | 18% | 3, 8, 9            |
| Frustrated              | 4 | 24% | 6, 19, 20, 21      |
| Distressed/desperate    | 4 | 24% | 7, 12, 17, 23      |
| Angry/suspicious        | 2 | 12% | 2, 4               |

**Finding:** 76% of users (13 of 17) showed indicators of emotional distress beyond neutral information-seeking. The system generates these states; users arrive at JustAnswer already struggling.

#### Material Consequences Evidenced in Threads

The threads document specific harms, not merely inconvenience:

**1. Appeal rights at risk**  
Nine threads (53%) involved users under appeal deadline pressure—needing transcripts while the 21-day clock was running or already in an appeal where transcripts were unavailable.

- Thread 7: "When much of court transcript relating to the oral evidence... is not obtainable can I request a retrial on the grounds of effectively lost my right of appeal?"
- Thread 4: User had "lodged an appeal" but received inaudible transcript after 4 months
- Thread 19: Expert advises "File N161 within 21 days. Order transcript via EX107"—two processes that cannot realistically complete within the same window

**2. Inability to verify what happened**  
Three threads (18%) involved recording problems that left users unable to prove what was said in court:

- Thread 4: "The transcriber said most of the recording was inaudible... content has been changed and added, altering the original meaning"
- Thread 2: "I suspect the judge may have withheld or altered the recording"
- Thread 7: Transcript "not obtainable"—appeal impaired

When recordings fail, users cannot challenge the court's account. Thread 4's user states: "I believe it should be criminal to alter court records." Whether or not alteration occurred, the user has no mechanism to verify.

**3. Cost discovered only after commitment**  
Thread 5's user asked a basic question: "How can I receive a transcript of the whole trial?" The expert's response revealed pricing the user did not know: "Transcription is charged per folio (72 words). Price depends on urgency."  
Thread 2's user had already paid ("I applied via EX107, paid") but received only partial delivery—cost incurred, transcript incomplete.

**4. Procedural limbo without escalation**  
- Thread 3: "It has been stuck on final approval for over a month. What does final approval mean, and why would it be stuck?"
- Thread 6: "The court acknowledged processing but never sent the audio. Now they are trying to stop me getting a crucial transcript."

In both cases, users had done everything correctly but faced opaque delays or arbitrary refusals with no escalation path.

#### What the Data Does Not Show

This sample cannot quantify:
- How many users abandoned attempts before seeking help
- Financial amounts paid or quoted (no specific figures except Thread 5's per-folio structure)
- Case outcomes (whether appeals succeeded or failed)
- Whether problems were eventually resolved

The threads capture moments of crisis, not complete journeys.

### 6.4.5.5 LiP-Specific Disadvantages

#### Explicit LiP Status in the Sample

Only **2 of the 17 directly relevant threads** explicitly stated that the user was a litigant in person:
- Thread 12: "We are unrepresented" (query about ex-partner sharing confidential documents)
- Thread 21: "No representation" (query on correcting errors in divorce judgment)

(Two further threads in the non-relevant sample also declared LiP status: Threads 11 & 14.)

#### Implied LiP Status

Despite only two explicit declarations, the **nature and level of the questions** across all 17 threads strongly indicate lack of professional support:
- Asking about basic forms ("Which form do I need?" — Thread 8)
- Asking about opaque process stages ("What does final approval mean?" — Thread 3)
- Asking questions a solicitor would routinely handle ("Can I appeal?" — Thread 19)
- Paying for online advice on procedural steps

Represented parties would normally have these matters resolved by their lawyer. The fact that users turned to a paid platform like JustAnswer is itself evidence of absent professional guidance.

#### What LiPs Did Not Know That Professionals Would

| What LiPs Asked                                      | What Professionals Already Know                                                                 |
|------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| "What does final approval mean?" (Thread 3)         | Judicial review/redaction stage; no statutory timeline; chase via court staff                   |
| "Which form do I need?" (Threads 5, 8, 9)           | EX107 for transcripts; N161 for appeal; N244 for general applications; when forms can be combined |
| "What if the recording is inaudible?" (Threads 4, 7)| Apply for judge's notes under PD30A ¶14; file appeal using notes if transcript approval stalled |
| "Can I use family court documents in civil proceedings?" (Thread 1) | Requires explicit judicial permission for release (Administration of Justice Act 1960 s.12) |
| "How do I appeal?" (Threads 17, 19, 21, 23)         | Order transcript **immediately** post-hearing if appeal is even possible; do **not** wait until the 21-day clock starts |

#### The Timing Disadvantage

In every appeal-related thread, experts advised: **"Order transcript via EX107"**—advice given **after** the adverse decision, when the 21-day (or 7-day) appeal window was already running.

A solicitor anticipates risk and orders the transcript **on the day of the hearing** (or sooner). LiPs discover the requirement only when they search for help, thereby losing days or weeks of the already short deadline.

#### What the Data Cannot Show

This sample cannot directly prove:
- Whether represented parties in similar situations succeed more often
- The overall proportion of LiP vs. represented queries on JustAnswer (platform selection bias)
- Outcomes for LiPs who never sought online help

The disadvantage is **inferential but clear**: LiPs repeatedly asked questions that any professional representative would already know the answer to, confirming a systemic knowledge and timing penalty for those without legal support.

## 6.4.6 Failure Points Mapped to Process Stages

### Process Flow and Barrier Concentration

Based on the coded data, problems cluster at two critical points:

**1. Post-hearing discovery (35% of threads)**  
Users learn they need transcripts only after receiving adverse decisions. By then, the 21-day appeal clock is running. Threads 17, 19, 20, 21, 23 show users asking "what can I do?" after the hearing, with experts advising them to order transcripts they should have requested earlier.

**2. Post-filing opacity (18% of threads)**  
Users who successfully filed EX107 encountered unexplained delays (Thread 3), arbitrary refusals (Thread 6), or incomplete delivery (Thread 2). The system provides no visibility into approval status, no escalation path, and no timeline.

### What the Flow Cannot Show

The sequential structure implies users move through discrete stages. In reality:
- Some users skip stages (Thread 5: general query, never filed)
- Some face multiple barriers simultaneously (Thread 4: delay + recording failure + appeal pressure)
- Some enter at crisis point (Thread 7: mid-appeal, transcript unavailable)

The map is an analytical tool, not a literal representation of user journeys.

## 6.4.7 Data Treatment and Anonymisation

Each JustAnswer thread was:
- **Logged:** URL, date/time, case context (anonymised)
- **Coded:** By process stage, barrier type, gap indicators
- **Mapped:** To official procedural steps and form guidance
- **Anonymised:** All identifying details removed; direct quotations used only where publicly posted

No contact was made with post authors or platform moderators. All data derives from publicly available forum posts.

## 6.4.9 Methodological Strengths and Limitations

### Diagnostic Validity Despite Small n
The n=17 directly relevant corpus achieves **thematic saturation**—patterns recur consistently, indicating genuine procedural breakdowns rather than isolated incidents. While not statistically representative of all transcript requests, the sample provides **unique diagnostic evidence** of design-reality gaps that official data sources cannot capture.

### JustAnswer as Necessity, Not Choice
The FOI data void (§6.1) means HMCTS holds **no statistics** on transcript processing, approval timelines, or refusal rates. JustAnswer queries therefore represent the **only systematically available evidence** of user experience navigating EX107 processes.

### Known Limitations
- **Selection bias:** Captures only users who (a) encounter problems, (b) seek help online, (c) use JustAnswer specifically
- **Survivorship bias:** Queries describe barriers encountered; cannot observe those who never attempted transcript access
- **Platform constraints:** Public forum may deter sensitive disclosures
- **Retrospective focus:** Describes problems post-hoc rather than tracking prospective journeys
- **Temporal clustering:** Sparse pre-2020 threads may reflect both platform usage and policy changes

### Why Patterns Remain Valid
When users report appeal deadline pressure (n=9, 53%), this **confirms** the structural collision documented in Layer 1. The **triangulation across layers** establishes that user experiences aren't anomalies but predictable consequences of documented design failures.

## 6.4.11 Layer 4 Contribution to the Five-Layer Framework

Layer 4 adds the user perspective, completing the evidence base before external validation:

- **Layer 1** (§6.1): What the system prescribes (institutional design)
- **Layer 2** (§6.2): What judicial gatekeeping permits (case law interpretation)
- **Layer 3** (§6.3): How professionals mediate (solicitor guidance patterns)
- **Layer 4** (§6.4): How users experience it (lived procedural reality)
- **Layer 5** (§6.5): External validation (independent observers, oversight bodies)

Layer 4 adds:
- **User voice** showing the system from the receiving end
- **Problem timing** revealing when confusion occurs (post-critical windows)
- **Knowledge gaps** exposing form communication failures
- **Material consequences** demonstrating harms (appeal jeopardy, financial exclusion)
- **LiP vulnerability** quantifying disadvantage vs. represented parties
